The four things we would rather you heard from us

Dates are still sugar. Fruit sugar with the fibre still attached, which is a genuinely different thing from a spoon of refined sugar, but sugar all the same. We add no refined sugars to the bites. We do not pretend the dates are not there.

These are calorie-dense on purpose. Nuts, seeds and ghee are energy-rich foods. Two bites are a snack. The box is not a serving.

They go off faster than a supermarket bar. There are no preservatives in any of the seven. A short shelf life is what that costs, and it is the honest trade.

We never print "100%" on anything. FSSAI asked food businesses to stop using it. We would rather print the actual percentages, which is what this page is.

Now go and read someone else's

The point is not that our lists are short. It is that you can now read any list the same way. Pick up whatever is in your kitchen right now and try it.

  1. Flip the pack over. Ignore the front entirely.
  2. Read ingredient number one. That is most of what is inside.
  3. Find the fat. Is it named (butter, ghee) or vague ("edible vegetable oil")?
  4. Find the sweetener, and check whether it appears more than once under different names.
  5. Count the additive and INS numbers. Nought to two is ordinary. A crowd is a factory product.

If a brand will not print percentages, that is information too.

Frequently asked questions

How do I read a food label quickly?

Use a fixed order rather than reading everything. Look at the first three ingredients, then count how many different sugar names appear, then check whether flavour is added or real, then count the INS additive codes, and finally read the "of which sugars" line per 100 g. Five checks, about a minute at first and roughly ten seconds once it is habit.

Why are the first three ingredients so important?

Ingredients are listed by weight, heaviest first, so the first three make up most of what is in the pack. If refined wheat flour, sugar and palm oil are the top three, that is what you are mainly eating, regardless of what the front of the packet claims. Everything listed after them is present in much smaller amounts.

What names does sugar hide under on Indian labels?

Common ones include liquid glucose, glucose syrup, dextrose, invert sugar, invert syrup, fructose, corn syrup, maltodextrin, fruit juice concentrate, malt extract and cane crystals. Because each name is weighed separately, splitting sugar across several of them keeps any single one from appearing near the top of the ingredient list.

Do I need to memorise INS numbers?

No. INS is simply an international code so the same additive is named identically everywhere, and many are harmless everyday substances such as baking soda or citric acid. Counting is far more useful than decoding: zero to two additives is ordinary, while a long run of them indicates a product engineered for shelf life, colour and texture.

What counts as a high sugar content per 100g?

As a rough guide, under 5 g per 100 g is genuinely low, 5 to 20 g is a middle band where portion size decides, and above roughly 20 to 25 g means the product is a sweet. The World Health Organization advises keeping free sugars under 10% of daily energy intake, and under 5% for additional benefit.

Practise it five times and it is yours

The first label takes a full minute and feels like homework. By the fifth, your eye jumps to the right three places on its own, and you will never really stop doing it. It is the rare habit that costs ten seconds and pays for years.
