Why Period Cramps Happen, Explained Simply

6 min read · 30 August 2026 · By Diet Swad

General information, not medical advice. If period pain is disrupting your life, see a doctor. Sources are listed at the end.

Period cramps are a muscle doing hard work with a restricted blood supply. The uterus is a muscle, it has to contract to shed its lining, and when those contractions get strong enough they briefly squeeze shut the vessels that feed the muscle itself. That momentary shortage of blood is what you feel.

Most explanations stop at "hormones", which is true but useless. The actual sequence is more specific, and knowing it makes several other things about a period suddenly make sense.

The sequence, step by step

  1. Hormone levels drop. If there is no pregnancy in a given cycle, progesterone falls away sharply. That fall is the starting gun for everything that follows.
  2. The lining releases prostaglandins. These are hormone-like chemicals made locally in tissue rather than in a gland. Their job here is to make the uterus contract.
  3. The uterus contracts. It has to, in order to shed the lining that built up over the previous weeks. This part is supposed to happen.
  4. Blood flow is briefly cut. Strong contractions compress the vessels supplying the uterine muscle. The tissue is short of oxygen for a moment.
  5. Nerves report it as pain. Oxygen-starved muscle releases substances that trigger pain signals. That is the cramp.

There is a useful comparison here. This is broadly the same reason a muscle cramps during hard exercise: it is working intensely with insufficient blood supply. Your uterus is doing an interval session and has not asked your permission.

The one idea worth keeping. More prostaglandins generally mean stronger contractions, and stronger contractions generally mean more pain. Research has repeatedly found higher prostaglandin levels in people with more painful periods. That single relationship explains most of what follows.

Why it is not only your stomach that hurts

Here is the part that answers a question a lot of people have never had answered: why does a period cause nausea, or loose motions, or a headache? Those are not the uterus.

Prostaglandins do not stay politely where they were made. Some enter the bloodstream and reach smooth muscle elsewhere in the body, including the gut. Smooth muscle responds to prostaglandins the same way wherever it is, which is by contracting.

So the gut cramps too. That is the nausea, the vomiting and the diarrhoea, and it is why those symptoms tend to peak on exactly the days the cramps are worst. It is one chemical signal reaching several places, not several separate problems.

The vocabulary, decoded

These words appear constantly and are rarely explained.

  • Dysmenorrhea simply means painful periods. That is the whole word.
  • Primary dysmenorrhea means period pain with no underlying condition causing it. The mechanism above, working as described.
  • Secondary dysmenorrhea means the pain is caused by something else, such as endometriosis or fibroids. This is precisely why persistent or worsening pain should be looked at rather than endured.
  • Prostaglandins are locally made chemical messengers. Not hormones from a gland, which is why they act where they are produced.
  • Myometrium is the muscular wall of the uterus, the muscle doing the contracting.

Where the tiredness comes from

Fatigue during a period usually has more than one cause stacked on top of another.

Blood loss depletes iron, and iron is what lets your blood carry oxygen. Over repeated cycles, particularly with heavy bleeding, that depletion accumulates and shows up as tiredness, breathlessness and difficulty concentrating. We cover the food side of that in iron-rich Indian foods and the vitamin C trick. Pain also disrupts sleep, and poor sleep makes pain feel worse the next day, which is an unhelpful loop. Hormonal shifts affect energy and mood on their own account.

If heavy bleeding and severe tiredness travel together for you, that combination is worth raising with a doctor specifically, because it can indicate anaemia, which is testable and treatable.

When to stop putting up with it

Period pain being common has quietly turned into period pain being ignored. Those are different things. Please see a doctor if:

  • Pain regularly stops you attending work, school or ordinary activities
  • It is getting worse over months or years rather than staying steady
  • Usual measures make no difference at all
  • Bleeding is very heavy, or lasts longer than seven days
  • You have pelvic pain at times other than your period
  • Pain began suddenly after years of ordinary periods

Bahut logon ko hota hai does not mean it should be dismissed. Common and normal are not synonyms, and a doctor is the right person to tell the difference.

Frequently asked questions

What causes period cramps?

Cramps are caused by the uterus contracting to shed its lining. Those contractions are driven by hormone-like substances called prostaglandins, which the uterine lining releases as hormone levels fall before a period. Strong contractions briefly squeeze the blood vessels supplying the uterine muscle, and that temporary reduction in blood flow is what registers as pain.

What does dysmenorrhea mean?

Dysmenorrhea is simply the medical word for painful periods. Primary dysmenorrhea means period pain that occurs without any underlying pelvic condition. Secondary dysmenorrhea means pain caused by a separate condition such as endometriosis or fibroids, which is one reason persistent or worsening pain should be assessed by a doctor.

Why do periods cause nausea and loose motions?

Prostaglandins do not stay in the uterus. Some enter the bloodstream and act on smooth muscle elsewhere in the body, including the gut. That is why nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea often arrive alongside cramps, and why they tend to be worst on the same days the cramps are worst.

Why am I so tired during my period?

Several things overlap. Menstrual blood loss depletes iron over time, and low iron reduces how much oxygen the blood carries, which causes fatigue. Pain interferes with sleep, and hormonal shifts affect energy and mood independently. Persistent heavy bleeding with severe tiredness is worth discussing with a doctor, since it can point to anaemia.

When should I see a doctor about period pain?

See a doctor if the pain regularly stops you doing normal activities, if it is getting worse over time, if it does not respond to usual measures, if bleeding is very heavy or lasts longer than seven days, or if pain occurs outside your period. Severe period pain is common but it is not something you simply have to endure without assessment.

Knowing why does help

It does not stop a cramp. But there is a real difference between pain that feels random and pain you can explain, and the second kind is easier to sit with.

It also makes you a better advocate for yourself in a doctor's room, because you can describe what is happening instead of apologising for it.
