Why the Same Brand Differs by Country

7 min read · 30 August 2026 · By Diet Swad

The same product name, the same logo, the same packet design, and a different recipe inside depending on which country you bought it in. This happens, it is documented, and it is not a conspiracy so much as the ordinary result of four separate forces pulling on the same product.

What follows is how those forces work, and what you can actually do about it while standing in a shop in India.

Four reasons a recipe changes at a border

They stack, which is why the differences can be larger than any one of them would explain.

  1. Regulation differs. What may be added, and how much of it, is set nationally. An additive permitted at one level in one country may be capped lower or not permitted at all in another. The recipe follows the rulebook it is sold under.
  2. Labelling rules differ. This one is subtler and matters more than people expect. Where a country forces a prominent front-of-pack warning above a sugar or salt threshold, manufacturers have a strong commercial reason to reformulate below it. Where no such label exists, that pressure is absent.
  3. Ingredient costs differ. Local prices for oils, sweeteners and flours vary considerably, and a recipe optimised for cost lands in a different place in each market. We go into the Indian numbers in why packaged food is so cheap.
  4. Taste and price expectations differ. Sweetness preferences genuinely vary between markets, and so does what shoppers will pay. Both push the formulation around.

The one that surprises people: a labelling rule can change a recipe without ever mentioning the recipe. Require a visible warning above a threshold, and products quietly move below the threshold. That is regulation working through commercial incentive rather than through prohibition.

Where India is actually stricter

The assumption that Indian standards are simply weaker is too broad, and worth correcting.

India caps industrial trans fat at 2% of total oils and fats, in force since 1 January 2022 after being phased down from 5%. That is among the stricter limits anywhere in the world, and it was a genuine public-health win that removed the single clearest problem in industrially produced fat.

India also requires ingredient lists in descending order by weight and mandatory allergen declaration, and it maintains the vegetarian and non-vegetarian mark, which most countries have no equivalent of at all.

Where India is currently behind

Chiefly in one place: front-of-pack labelling.

Several countries now require a clear warning on the front when a product exceeds thresholds for sugar, salt or saturated fat. India does not, yet. FSSAI proposed an Indian Nutrition Rating star system in draft in 2022, intended to be voluntary and then mandatory, and it has not come into force. In August 2026 the Supreme Court directed FSSAI to finalise the rules, with the bench questioning the delay directly.

This matters more than a single additive limit would, because front-of-pack labelling is the rule that changes recipes at scale. Its absence is why the back of the pack is currently doing all the work in India.

The children's-food question

This is the version of the story most parents have seen. Public-interest organisations have published investigations reporting that products aimed at infants and young children contained added sugar in some markets while equivalent products in other markets did not, and regulators have examined those findings.

We are deliberately not naming companies here. Partly because it is not our place to litigate a specific case in a blog, and partly because naming one brand implies the others are fine, which is exactly the wrong lesson. The useful takeaway is structural: where a market does not require something to be flagged, it will not always be volunteered. For anything you feed an infant, read the ingredient list yourself and take your paediatrician's advice.

What to actually do in an Indian shop

You cannot compare formulations across countries while standing in a kirana. You can do this instead, and it takes half a minute.

  1. Read the first three ingredients. Descending by weight, so those three are most of the product. This is the whole method, and it works regardless of what any international comparison says.
  2. Find the "of which sugars" line. In grams per 100 g. A teaspoon is about 4 g. This number cannot be dressed up.
  3. Check what the front claims and whether the back agrees. If the front says almond, find the almond in the list and see where it sits.
  4. Count the additives. Not to fear them individually, but as a gauge of how engineered the product is. See what preservatives are and what they do.
  5. For anything for a child, apply all four properly. This is the category where it matters most and where the marketing is loudest.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the same product have a different recipe in different countries?

Several reasons operate together. Local regulation sets different limits on what may be added, labelling rules differ in what must be declared and how prominently, ingredient costs vary by market, and recipes are adjusted to local taste and price expectations. A product sold in two countries is often two formulations sharing one name.

Are Indian food standards weaker than European ones?

Not uniformly. India's cap on industrial trans fat at 2% of total oils and fats is among the stricter limits in the world. Where India currently lags is front-of-pack labelling, which several countries already require and which FSSAI has proposed but not yet brought into force. Standards differ area by area rather than one country being simply stricter.

How can I check what is actually in a product sold in India?

Read the ingredient list on the back, which is printed in descending order by weight, and check the of which sugars line in the nutrition panel. Those two together tell you what the product mostly is and how much sugar it carries, regardless of what the front of the pack or any international comparison says.

Do baby and children's foods differ between markets?

Investigations by public-interest organisations have reported cases where products aimed at infants and young children contained added sugar in some markets while equivalent products elsewhere did not. Regulators have examined such findings. For any product intended for an infant, read the ingredient list directly and follow your paediatrician's guidance rather than a general article.

Does front-of-pack labelling exist in India?

Not yet in force. FSSAI proposed an Indian Nutrition Rating star system in draft form in 2022, and the Supreme Court has since directed FSSAI to finalise front-of-pack labelling. Until it arrives, the back of the pack is the only place carrying reliable information.

The label is the leverage

It is easy to read this and feel that nothing is in your control. Mostly the opposite is true: the specific thing that changes recipes at scale is a rule about what has to be printed on the front, and that rule is currently being argued over in public.

Until it lands, the back of the pack is where the truth lives. Which is the same conclusion we keep arriving at, from a different direction.
